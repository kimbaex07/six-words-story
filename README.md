> Her letter arrived. He'd already moved.

## Why it works

Six words, one whole story — and the story turns on a single word: *already*.

The first sentence builds a small hope. A letter has arrived, which means someone wrote it, someone carried it, someone waited for it. The second sentence takes that hope away: the man it was meant for is gone before the words reach him. The turn is the timing — the letter was not refused, it was simply too late, and "already" is the sting that makes the lateness feel irreversible.

Every word earns its place. "Her" gives the letter a sender with feelings in it, not just a sender. "Arrived" carries the long journey behind the envelope. "Already" does the emotional work of the whole line, marking the moment the hope dies. And "moved" does double duty: he changed address, and he moved on — two kinds of leaving in a single word, so the reader cannot tell which one hurts more.

What is left unsaid is everything the reader most wants to know: what the letter said, why she wrote it so late, whether he will ever learn it came, and whether she will write again to an address that no longer holds him. The six words sketch the outline; the reader fills in a whole relationship — the parting, the silence, the letter written too late, the empty house at the end of it. Read aloud, it lands in two beats, a rise and a fall, like a door opening and closing.

## Run it

```bash
python3 main.py
```

This writes the story to `/output/entry.txt` (UTF-8, trailing newline).
